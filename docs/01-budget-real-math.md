# Chapter 1: The Real Math — Budgeting for Your First Place

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the advertised rent is not the cost of the apartment.
It's the appetizer. This chapter shows you how to compute the **real monthly number**
before you fall in love with a place you can't afford.

---

## 1.1 The rule everyone cites (and what it actually means)

You've heard "don't spend more than 30% of your income on rent." Here's how to apply it
without guesswork:

- It usually refers to **gross income** (before taxes), not take-home pay.
- On take-home pay, that same rent is often closer to **40%** — which is why the 30%
  rule can feel broken even when you follow it.
- Practical version: **aim for rent ≤ 30% of gross / ~40% of take-home**, and treat
  anything well above that as a "I must have a roommate" flag, not a dealbreaker.

## 1.2 The move-in bill (the one that ambushes people)

Most first-time renters plan for rent and forget the mountain due on day one. Typical
move-in costs:

| Item | Typical amount |
|---|---|
| First month's rent | 1 × monthly rent |
| Security deposit | 1 × rent (up to 2+ in some markets; some are smaller) |
| Application fee | $25–$75 **per adult applicant** (varies) |
| Admin/move-in fees | $0–$500, building-dependent |
| Pet deposit/fees | $0–$600+ if you have a pet |
| Renter's insurance | $12–$30/month, often required, usually payable annually |
| Utility deposits/activation | $0–$200 per utility (electric, water, internet) |
| Moving costs | $0 (friend-and-borrowed-van) to $1,000+ (movers) |

### Example: $1,400/mo apartment

Worst-realistic case for a solo renter with a pet:

```
First month + deposit      $2,800
Application + fees (you)   $100
Pet deposit                $300
Renter's insurance (year)  $180
Utility setup              $100
Moving (DIY, van + pizza)  $150
                          ------
Move-in total             ~$3,630
```

That's **$3,600+ before you've unpacked a single box**. Save toward this number first.

## 1.3 The real monthly number

Take the rent and stack on the recurring costs people forget:

```
RENT                              $1,400
Renter's insurance                 $15
Electric                           $80  (varies hugely with season + heater/AC)
Water/sewer/trash                  $40  (sometimes billed separately by landlord)
Internet                           $50
Gas (heat/cooking)                 $30
Subscriptions/streaming            $25
Parking/garage                     $50  (or transit pass — your version)
                                  ------
Real monthly                      ~$1,790
```

That $1,790 — not $1,400 — is the number that has to fit in your budget. Many people
compute only the sticker rent and feel "fine" until month three, when the electric bill
plus the auto-renewed streaming services plus a parking charge should have done this
math for them. Now it has.

## 1.4 The 50/30/20 sanity check (adapted for renters)

A common starting framework — adapt it, don't worship it, and it's a *guideline only*,
not a rule with consequences:

- **50% needs:** all housing costs (the real number), minimum debt payments, groceries,
  phone, transportation to work.
- **30% wants:** eating out, fun, hobbies.
- **20% savings & safety:** emergency fund, retirement, any debt above minimums.

If housing eats 60% of your take-home, something in the other two buckets gets squeezed.
That's when you consider a roommate (Chapter 5), a farther-out location with a longer
commute, or a smaller place. All three are normal first-apartment moves. None of them
are failures.

## 1.5 Build your emergency buffer (rent version)

Three quiet rules that save first-time renters:

1. **Have one full month of the *real* number saved before you move.** If a job change
   or surprise hits in month two, you can pay rent without panic.
2. **Keep a "boring stuff" fund** — $500–$1,000 for deposits, setup hiccups, and a
   first grocery run (dishes, shower curtain, toilet plunger... it adds up faster
   than you think).
3. **Rent comes first among bills.** If money gets tight in a given month, rent and
   food outrank everything else. A missed utility payment costs a late fee; a missed
   rent payment starts a formal landlord process that is much worse.

## 1.6 The budget worksheet (do this on paper or a notes app)

Fill in your own numbers before apartment hunting:

```
Monthly take-home pay (after taxes): $______
Hours/gig/second-job income:         $______ (be honest, use the LOW month)

Real monthly rent + utilities:       $______  ← target ≤ ~40% of take-home
Transport to work/school:            $______
Groceries:                           $______
Phone:                               $______
Student loan / car / credit minimums:$______
Renter's insurance:                  $______
Everything fun:                      $______  ← keep some, you're a person
                                     --------
Left over for savings:               $______  ← want this ≥ $100 to start

Move-in cash needed (rule of thumb):  3 × rent + $500
Do you have it? ______ If no: what's the gap, and what closes it
(roommate? smaller unit? two more months of saving?)
```

If the left-over number is negative on your *low* income month, that's not a moral
failing — it's just the sign that this particular apartment (or this particular solo
plan) doesn't fit yet. Try Chapter 5 on roommates; splitting a $1,400 place two ways
changes every line above.

## 1.7 Cost creeps to watch after you sign

- **Utilities in winter/summer:** electric can double in extreme months. Budget the
  *worst* season, not the average.
- **Auto-adds:** delivery apps, new streaming trials. They individually look small —
  the sum is a bill you didn't budget.
- **Transit vs. parking trade:** a cheaper apartment 25 extra minutes away can quietly
  cost more in gas/parking/tolls than it saves in rent. Do the commute math per month.
- **Amenities you pay for and don't use:** a $250 "convenience fee" for a gym you visit
  twice is just expensive rent.

## 1.8 Blunt advice section, because you deserve it

- If a listing is *suspiciously* cheap, ask why before applying. Sometimes it's a
  genuine deal; more often the fine print, location, or condition explains it.
- Never pay a "hold the unit" deposit to see the place — see Chapter 2 for spotting
  lister scams.
- Any apartment budget that only works if nothing ever goes wrong isn't a budget;
  it's a hope. Add the buffer.
