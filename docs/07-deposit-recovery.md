# Chapter 7: Getting Your Security Deposit Back

The deposit is your money, temporarily held. Getting it back is not luck — it's a
process, typically with a legal deadline, that rewards the tenant who does six small
things on a schedule. This chapter is that schedule.

> **Not legal advice:** deposit law is *the* most location-variable topic in this
> manual. States set maximum amounts, required holding arrangements, return deadlines
> (commonly 14–45 days after move-out), itemization requirements, interest rules, and
> penalties for missing them. Before arguing about any of it, look up your own state's
> deposit rules — your state's official consumer/tenant page or a local legal-aid site
> will state the deadline and the deduction rules in two paragraphs. This chapter
> gives you the process and paperwork that works regardless of the exact numbers.

---

## 7.1 The timeline (print this mentally)

| When | What you do |
|---|---|
| 30–60 days before move-out | Give written notice as the lease requires. Re-read the deposit clause (Ch. 3.4). |
| 2–3 weeks out | Deep clean; plan repairs. Book the move-out walkthrough. |
| 1 week out | Cartons done, walls patched if the lease asks, photos taken. |
| Move-out day | Full walkthrough with landlord if possible; keys returned; condition form signed; forwarding address **in writing**. |
| Deadline (per your state) | Deposit + itemized statement due back from the landlord. |
| If it doesn't arrive | Polite dated inquiry (7.4), then further escalation if needed. |

## 7.2 The deep clean with checklist (worth actual money)

Deposit deductions most commonly claimed: cleaning fees, wall damage, carpet, and
"missing items." The clean is cheap; the claimed cleaning fee is not. Your blitz list:

- **Kitchen:** oven and stovetop (including the little burners and the knob-grease
  halo), fridge inside/out and *behind*, microwave, counters, cabinet fronts
  (grease gets high — top edge of doors), sink and faucet shined, floors mopped,
  and the filter thing nobody cleans (range hood filter, dishwasher filter if any).
- **Bathrooms:** toilet (base, brackets, behind), tub/shower (drain visible and
  clear, grout scrubbed), mirror, vanity inside/out, exhaust-fan cover, floors.
- **Every room:** walls — dust marks, pencil holes filled (if lease expects),
  scuff spots wiped with the approved method; baseboards; window sills and tracks;
  blinds; light fixtures and switch plates; closets empty and wiped.
- **Floors:** vacuum edges (the line that betrays skipped vacuuming), then carpet
  spot-treat; hard floors followed by mop. Many leases require *professional carpet
  cleaning* with receipt — do that if yours says so, and keep the receipt.
- **The scrutinized spots:** behind/under the fridge and washer-dryer hookups (lint
  and accumulated debris), balcony/patio, entry closet, the top of the
  fridge, door tops and door frames.

Photos **after** cleaning, whole room wide shots + close-ups of the formerly
problematic spots (the ones from your move-in photos). These "after" shots pair
with your move-in shots and end arguments.

## 7.3 The move-out walkthrough (with the landlord if you can)

Ask in advance for a joint inspection on a specific date; many landlords will.
Bring: your move-in photos, the condition form, your repair log (Ch. 6.3), a pen.

- Walk room by room against the move-in form. Anything landlord-noted gets written
  down verbatim, plus your response in the form ("stain present at move-in — see
  photo 14, 2026-06-01").
- Get signatures on the form if offered; take photos of each signed page.
- **Return all keys, fobs, remotes;** photograph them in the landlord's hand isn't
  feasible — photograph them in a pile and note the return time in your log ("keys
  returned to office 5:40pm, clerk's name: ___").
- **Leave your forwarding address in writing** (this often starts the legal clock
  for the deposit return — a formally required step in many states).

If the landlord won't do a joint walkthrough, do your own solo version anyway, at
the same hour you would have, take the same photos, and email the lot that day:
"Self-conducted move-out walkthrough photos — [address] — [date]. Forwarding
address: [___]." A tenant who *can't get* the joint meeting but behaves as if one
happened is in the strongest position there is.

## 7.4 If the deposit is short, late, or silent

**The first inquiry (dated, calm, copies kept):**

```
Subject: Security deposit — [address, unit] — move-out date [date]

Hi [name],

I vacated [address] on [date] and provided a forwarding address the same
day. As of [state deadline date], I haven't received the deposit or an
itemized statement of deductions.

Could you send both this week? My records (move-in and move-out photos,
signed condition form, repair log) are attached for reference.

Thanks,
[new address, phone]
```

Then, in order, with dates in your log:

1. **Follow-up** in 7–10 days by the same and one different channel (email + office
   call with name of who you spoke to).
2. **Written demand** (short, firm): cite the amount, the move-out date, the
   state-deadline concept as *your understanding*, and say what comes next: "If not
   resolved by [date], I'll pursue the available remedies." (In many states that
   means small-claims court; some states provide penalty multipliers or attorney
   fees for bad-faith withholding — your local resource is the source for what
   "penalties" means exactly.)
3. **Small claims / free help:** many cities offer free tenant hotlines and clinics;
   legal-aid orgs help with exactly this; small-claims filings are designed for
   self-representation. Bring: this chapter's exhibit list.

## 7.5 The exhibit list (what wins deposit disputes)

Organized in one folder, digitally:

- [ ] Lease + all addenda + house rules (signed copy)
- [ ] Move-in condition form + move-in photos (dated)
- [ ] Repair log (Chapter 6.3 table) + copies of every request/dispatch record
- [ ] Rent receipts/statements or payment-app history for the full tenancy
- [ ] Move-out notice letter (with proof: email or certified receipt)
- [ ] Move-out "after" photos (dated)
- [ ] Signed move-out form/photos of it — or your walkthrough email when joint
      wasn't offered
- [ ] Forwarding-address proof (in writing, dated)
- [ ] Carpet-cleaning receipt if lease required it
- [ ] All deposit inquiries and replies (screenshots count)

Name the files so a stranger could audit them in order: `01_lease.pdf`,
`02_movein_form.pdf`, `03_movein_photos_2026-06-01/`, and so on. When you're calm and
organized, you are — functionally — the one holding the receipts. Which is the point.

## 7.6 Fair deductions vs. scare tactics: what "normal wear and tear" means

The phrase that decides nearly every deposit debate. A rough, common-sense scale
(again, your local rules and your lease's wording govern the actual outcome):

- **Usually fair deductions:** damage clearly *you* caused and the cost to repair —
  a hole in the drywall, broken window from a thrown object, burnt countertop,
  pet pee-ruined carpet pad, missing keys/fobs (replacement cost), excessive filth
  beyond normal use.
- **Usually *not* a fair deduction ("normal wear and tear"):** faded paint, sun
  fading, nail holes from reasonable hanging, gently scuffed floors, worn carpet
  path in a hallway, small marks needing a magic-eraser touch-up, aging appliance
  that dies of age.
- **The classic test:** a careful new tenant moving in would expect... this state?
  If the answer is yes, it's wear. If no — if a new tenant would gasp — it's likely
  damage.
- **Depreciation logic:** landlords sometimes claim full replacement cost for things
  that were already old. Ask the age. A 10-year-old carpet replaced at full cost off
  *your* deposit is a common overreach; many places prorate by remaining life. Ask
  (in writing) how the charge was computed — itemization should show amounts, and
  vague "$500 damage" with zero evidence is a challengeable claim.

## 7.7 Roommates and the deposit split

When roommates moved in separately and leave separately, deposits get scrambled. The
ground rules that keep it civil:

- The deposit belongs to the tenancy, not any one person — splits were agreed in the
  roommate agreement (Ch. 5.2). The landlord returns to one ledger (usually to
  whoever's on the lease/forwarded); the split is your problem, not theirs — budget
  the *handoff*, not the fight.
- **Departing roommates:** your share comes from the incoming person's deposit at
  move-in (Ch. 5.3) — the date it's paid is the date everyone agreed in writing.
- For the last person out: keep the whole-return pipeline moving with the 7.4 ladder,
  then distribute per the agreement. Photos of the *final* condition are everyone's
  insurance; the last person out is the one holding them, so take them.
