# Chapter 2: Applications and Credit Talk

You've found a place you like and the math works (Chapter 1 — you did do the math,
right?). Now you have to get *approved*. This chapter covers what's on a rental
application, how your credit gets checked, what landlords look for, and how to handle
the "my credit is rough" scenario without embarrassment.

> **Not legal advice:** screening rules vary by state and city — some places cap
> application fees, some require disclosure of screening criteria, and fair-housing
> rules protect applicants in every location from discrimination. Ask to see the
> landlord's written *screening criteria*; every question below has a plain-English
> answer there.

---

## 2.1 What's on a typical application

Expect to provide:

- **Identity:** legal name, date of birth, current and previous addresses (2–3 years).
- **ID:** driver's license or government ID number.
- **Income:** pay stubs (usually 2–3), an offer letter, tax returns for
  self-employed folks, or benefit statements.
- **Employment:** employer name and contact info.
- **Rental history:** current landlord's name and phone.
- **References:** sometimes.
- **Consent:** a signature allowing a credit and background check.
- **Fees:** an application fee, commonly $25–$75 per adult (18+). Fees vary, and some
  jurisdictions cap them. Group applications: ask if the fee is per *applicant* or
  per *unit* before you and two friends each pay $50.

**Bring/have ready before you apply:**

- [ ] Two or three recent pay stubs
- [ ] Photo ID
- [ ] Current landlord's contact info (permission to give it as a reference)
- [ ] A short list of reference contacts
- [ ] Bank statement or two (some landlords)
- [ ] A blank check or bank routing info for rent autopay setup (later, not at apply)

## 2.2 Credit talk, plain English

A credit report is basically a history of how you've handled borrowed money and bills.
A credit **score** is a number summarizing it. Landlords usually pull a softer version
than a mortgage lender and mostly look for *patterns*, not perfection.

What matters most to a landlord, roughly in order:

1. **Eviction history** — the single biggest red flag for any landlord.
2. **Open collections from landlords or utilities** — signals "doesn't settle up."
3. **Recent serious delinquency** — months of unpaid accounts.
4. **Overall account pattern** — paid-on-time history, even on small accounts (phone
   plan, student loan) counts in your favor.
5. **Income ratio** — often "gross monthly income ≥ 2.5–3× rent." Not credit, but
   usually paired with it in the written criteria.

Things a landlord *typically* does not care much about (but check their written
criteria): your exact score (many use minimums like 620/650, but small landlords just
read the report yourself), how much you owe on a car loan if you've paid reliably, or
checking accounts you never opened credit with.

## 2.3 "My credit is thin or rough" — the honest playbook

Having a thin or rough credit history is extremely normal in your first apartment years.
Here's what actually helps, in order of usefulness:

1. **Apply with a cosigner or guarantor.** A cosigner (usually a parent or relative)
   signs the lease promising to pay if you don't. It's a real commitment for them —
   explain it that way — and it's the most common fix. Many buildings offer formal
   "guarantor services" too (they charge; read their terms).
2. **Offer a larger deposit** *where allowed*. Some states cap how much deposit a
   landlord may take, so ask before offering — "is a larger deposit acceptable under
   your criteria?" works even where it's not.
3. **Add proof of steady income and a savings cushion.** Bank statements showing 2–3
   months of rent saved mitigates a lot.
4. **Show recent good behavior.** Six months of paid-on-time utility/phone bills you
   can evidence (screenshots/statements) tells a story.
5. **Write a short, honest letter.** Two or three sentences: what happened (job loss,
   medical bill, first-ever credit mistake), what's different now, why you'll pay on
   time. Landlords are people; a brief honest note beats silence.
6. **Consider a smaller independent landlord.** Small "for rent by owner" landlords
   often weigh a conversation and references more heavily than big
   property-management-style screening.

And the long game: paying *everything* on time — including that phone bill — is how a
thin credit file becomes a solid one. There's no overnight fix, which is exactly why
starting now matters.

## 2.4 Spotting listing scams (this can cost you money *before* you rent)

Rental scams have a signature. Red flags:

- **Rent well below market for the neighborhood.** Good deal ≠ scam, but a suspicious
  deal deserves a suspicious eye.
- **"I'm the owner but I'm out of the country — wire me a deposit to hold it."**
  Never wire money to see a place. Never send a deposit before you've seen the unit
  (in person or a live video walkthrough where *their* camera moves at your direction).
- **Refuses any live meeting/in-person showing.** Time-zone excuses stacked on excuses.
- **The address doesn't exist or the photos are Oceanfront Property in Nowhere.**
  Reverse-image-search the photos; if they show a different city's rental, walk.
- **Asks for application fee info via text with no application form.** Real screening
  goes through a process, not a payment-app request.
- **Pressure + urgency:** "Three other applications in the works, need $2,000 *right
  now*." Healthy landlords lose applicants to slowness all the time and still exist.

Golden rule of the pre-lease stage: **no money changes hands before an identity match**
(you've met/been shown the actual unit, matched the person to the property records,
and gotten a lease to review).

## 2.5 Filling out the application well

- **Be accurate and consistent.** The address you write must match your credit report's
  history. Mismatched info slows everything; *misstated* income is documentable fraud.
- **Round income down, never up.** They'll verify with pay stubs anyway.
- **Give your current landlord a heads-up:** "I'm applying at X, they may call — please
  give an honest reference." A surprised reference call reads worse than a middling one.
- **Apply fast but don't rush.** Good units go quickly. Preparation (Section 2.1's
  checklist) is how you're fast *and* accurate.

## 2.6 Approval and beyond — the small print between "yes" and "sign"

Once approved, expect:

1. **Approval email/letter** with conditions (e.g., "route through guarantor service").
2. **The lease to review.** Do not sign the same hour you receive it — Chapter 3 is a
   clause-by-clause read-through for exactly this step.
3. **Payment instructions** for deposit/first month — verify these are the official
   property payment channels, not a personal payment app to a stranger.
4. **A move-in date and walkthrough.** Chapter 4 turns that walkthrough into your
   money-shield.

If a *different* unit than the one you toured is "what's available," clarify in writing
which unit/address your application and lease cover. Vague unit-handing is a real (and
avoidable) headache.
