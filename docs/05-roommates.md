# Chapter 5: Roommates (Money, Agreements, and Conflict — Early)

Roommates are the cheapest financial product ever invented: a contract between
friends that halves your rent. They work great about 90% of the time, and the
remaining 10% is where the agreements below earn keep.

> **Not legal advice.** Co-tenancy rules, sublet rules, and eviction process all vary
> by location. The fix is boring and reliable: a written agreement plus your local
> housing resources for the serious stuff.

---

## 5.1 Co-tenants vs. "one person's name on the lease"

The single most important roommate decision, made *before* move-in:

1. **Co-tenants (everyone signs the lease):** everyone has equal rights. All are
   jointly responsible for the *full* rent to the landlord — if one leaves, the
   landlord bills the rest for the shortfall. Strong, clean, slightly scary
   (that's what the roommate agreement is for).
2. **One tenant + approved occupants:** legally, the person on the lease carries the
   whole lease; roommates are guests-with-loyalty. Rents are more flexible; exit
   rights depend entirely on the agreement you write.
3. **Master-tenant + sublet:** one person is the landlord's tenant; you're their
   tenant. Fine for school years, but your "landlord" is a 22-year-old, and their
   memory is also just a memory. (Same advice: write it down.)

Whatever you pick: **know which you picked.** Half of roommate blowups are people
with different assumptions about option 1 vs 2.

## 5.2 The roommate agreement (one page, now, not later)

Not a lease — an agreement among roommates. Write it *before* moving in, while
everyone is trying to be nice. Fill in:

- **Rent and deposit split** — usually by room size; unequal rooms deserve unequal
  numbers and everyone saying so out loud.
- **Utilities split and who pays which bill** — and the day the Venmo/Zelle/cash-app
  is *due* (e.g., "power by the 10th, internet by the 5th").
- **Shared-supply plan** — paper towels, dish soap, trash bags: a joint shopping list
  with a rotation beat-by-beat ("rotating buyers each month") outperforms clever apps.
- **Chores** — a short list with owners, not a manifesto. Two items each, on a
  visible sheet, works better than you'd think.
- **Quiet hours + guests** — overnight-guest frequency you can all live with, in
  writing, was The Reason for 70% of past conflicts. Solve it while you're friends.
- **How either of you can exit** — replacement found to take their room, notice
  period (30 days is normal), deposit handoff timing, who's responsible if the
  replacement flakes.
- **What happens to shared stuff** — the TV, the couch, the spatula rotation.
  List it. It sounds petty; it's the actual thing people email about later.

Sign or initial it, everyone keeps a copy (photo counts). **One-digit roommate conflicts
that start with "we never really talked about it" are completely avoidable** — this
page is the avoiding.

## 5.3 Money rules that prevent 80% of roommate fights

- **Separate payments, always.** Never have one roommate pay the whole rent and the
  others pay them *later*. When someone is short, the paying person becomes both bank
  and enemy. If one person *must* pay (portal limitations), add a hard rule: "we each
  transfer our share by the 1st; transfers are receipts."
- **Every shared bill is two-way visible.** Whoever holds the account shares access
  or screenshots. Mystery = conflict.
- **Late is a conversation on day one, not day 30.** "I'm short this month, here's my
  plan, here's when it's paid" is a functioning adult message; silence is what
  destroys friendships.
- **Deposit flows both ways.** When someone leaves, the new roommate replaces their
  deposit share at move-in — not "we'll sort it out later." Write the handoff date.

## 5.4 Choosing (and being) a decent roommate

Green flags in a candidate:

- Talks about money without drama (ask directly: "what's your take on splitting
  utilities?").
- Has a job/source of income with some stability.
- Answers reference questions from a *former* roommate cheerfully.
- Says what they need ("I need quiet by 11" is a feature, not a flaw).

Red flags — soft ones, worth a pause:

- Vague about *how* they'll pay ("stuff's coming from an investor deal...").
- Wants to move in *today* with zero plan (same urgency-scam energy as Chapter 2).
- Won't put things in writing, frames writing as distrust. It isn't; it's kindness
  to future-you.

Be willing to *be* the roommate who writes things down; ironically, that's the
person everyone wants to live with.

## 5.5 When someone wants out (early warning + decent exits)

Warning signs the arrangement is wobbling: bills sliding past due dates, silent
weeks, doors doubling as walls. Talk earlier than feels necessary — "hey, things
feel off, want to reset on chores/bills?" is printable exact text you may use.

If someone's leaving:

1. **Read the lease's exit rules** (Chapter 3.9) — the landlord's rules come first.
2. **Replacement first** — the cleanest exits replace the person: find someone,
   get landlord approval if required, *then* set the exit date.
3. **Deposit handoff in writing** — date, amount, from whom to whom. No "the new
   guy will get me back... eventually."
4. **The joint-liability truth:** until the lease is amended/reassigned, a departed
   co-tenant may still be on the hook — and those *remaining* are on the hook for
   their shortfall. which is why steps 1–3 matter *so much.*

## 5.6 If it goes bad (conflict, not lawsuits)

The escalation ladder:

1. **One direct, calm conversation.** Name the issue, not the person's character.
2. **Written follow-up.** Two sentences summarizing what was agreed. This makes it
   real and limits re-litigating the conversation later.
3. **Mediation.** Many cities offer free/low-cost community dispute resolution;
   universities have ombuds offices; some landlords mediate building disputes.
4. **Local help.** For true habitability, safety, or money-owed disputes, your
   local legal-aid housing resources beat any manual, *including this one*.
5. **Exit via the lease/roommate agreement's own terms.** It's almost always
   cheaper than the anger-shaped alternative, whatever your state's details are.

Roommates will be forgotten in ten years except as stories. The rule "read the
money clauses *now* while everybody's nice" is what decides whether the stories
are funny or expensive.
