# Chapter 6: Repairs and Records (The Paper Trail That Pays)

Landlords fix things *faster for tenants with records*, and deposits survive with
tenants who have receipts. Same habit powers both. This chapter is the smallest-effort,
highest-return chapter in the manual.

> **Not legal advice.** Repair obligations and timelines vary heavily by state/city —
> some places give tenants formal "repair and deduct" rights, some require going
> through housing authorities, some require specific notice forms. For the legal
> mechanics, use your local housing/tenant resources. For the *records habit*, use
> this chapter anywhere on Earth.

---

## 6.1 The two-minute repair-request template

Save this in your notes app. Copy, edit, send. Works via email or any portal's
"message" field.

```
Subject: Repair request — [unit #] — [issue, one line] — [date]

Hi [property manager name],

Reporting a maintenance issue at [address, unit #]:
[One or two sentences: what's wrong, where, since when, any safety concern.]

Photos attached. Please let me know the expected timing. If you'd like
more access, I'm generally available [your windows].

Thanks,
[Name, unit #, phone]
```

Then, whatever the reply is, **keep it.** Screenshots of portal messages, forwarded
emails, photos of sticky notes if that's what happens. The fix log is the asset.

## 6.2 What to report immediately vs. eventually

**Same-day report (water and safety love speed):**

- Any leak, drip, or water where water shouldn't be (ceiling stains, damp baseboards)
- No heat in cold months / no hot water / no running water
- Anything electrical that sparks, smells hot, or trips breakers repeatedly
- Broken exterior door, lock, or window that won't latch/lock
- Pest sightings (one roach is data; three is a report)
- Smoke/CO detector beeping or dead
- Mold smell/visible growth, especially near water sources

**Report within a few days:** slow drains, sticking windows, small appliance
problems, cracked outlet covers, missing screens.

**Report eventually (before move-out, at the latest):** cosmetic stuff that
predates you (in fact you should've documented it at move-in — Chapter 4) or minor
niggles you can live with. Everything you *notice* is worth a note in your log; the
notification timeframe is the only real category of urgency.

## 6.3 The repair log (a table you'll thank yourself for)

One note per apartment, a running table:

| Date | Issue | Requested how | Reply | Fixed date | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-06-12 | Kitchen faucet drips | Email + 2 photos | 6/14 "tech scheduled" | 6/17 | valves replaced |
| 2026-07-03 | Bathroom fan loud | Portal msg | 7/5 "will look" | 7/11 | motor swapped |

Five columns. Two minutes per entry. This table is what Chapter 7 reads when the
deposit is being argued about — and what turns "they never fixed anything!" into a
factual, dated list no one can wave away.

## 6.4 The follow-nudge ladder (being politely relentless)

When a fix doesn't happen on a normal timescale (days for safety/essential, a week
or two for the rest):

1. **Day 0:** report (Section 6.1).
2. **Day ~5:** friendly nudge to the same channel: "Checking on this — timing?"
3. **Day ~10:** nudge + mention you're keeping a log: "Still open; I'm logging
   requests and responses. Is there an ETA?" (This is not hostile; it's bookkeeping.)
4. **Day ~14–21:** escalate channels: call the office, ask for the property
   manager's name, and (if your building has one) ask how the owner/management
   company wants overflow issues routed. Some cities have housing/rental inspection
   offices for exactly this; a polite inquiry to your local housing authority or
   code-enforcement office is a legitimate next rung for serious habitability issues.
5. **Throughout:** every contact gets a calendar note with date + summary.

The tone that works: short, dated, factual, un-panicked. The tone that backfires:
long essays, caps lock, or anything you'd be embarrassed to read aloud in the office.

## 6.5 Tenant-side maintenance you actually own

Your lease (Ch. 3.6) says some upkeep is yours. The normal ones, with rough rhythms:

- **HVAC/furnace filter:** check monthly, replace roughly every 1–3 months (if
  the lease says tenant-supplied, they're ~$10; pet-heavy homes need the shorter end).
- **Smoke/CO detector batteries:** test the button monthly (a literal one-minute
  chore; some buildings handle residentally); replace batteries you're responsible
  for when it chirps.
- **Drain care:** hair strainers ($3, saves three plumber-visits' worth of discourse)
  and no-fat-down-the-sink. If it backs up anyway — report, don't DIY-drill.
- **Caulk keeps water where it belongs:** if bathroom caulk peels, report early
  rather than paint-fixing; peeling caulk is how hidden rot starts, and rot repairs
  are landlord territory *when they're reported*.
- **Refrigerator drip pan/coils:** some leases ask the tenant to keep coils clean —
  a vacuum nozzle once or twice a year.
- **Fridge water line / icemaker** leaks: report at first drip, not after the
  floor buckles.

The deal: landlords pay for *systems* and *structure*; you pay for habitable living
and *reporting*. When in doubt about which side something lands on, note the lease
language, and ask in writing — the reply itself becomes a record.

## 6.6 Photographs: the recurring habit

Monthly, ten minutes: walk the apartment with your camera and take its "temperature":

- Under every sink (dry? damp?)
- Bathroom ceiling and window corners
- Water heater area + laundry hookups
- Window sills (condensation stains, mold freckles)
- The one corner where *stuff* piles up (dampness hides under piled things)

Photo anything new and report it. This habit is what catches the $50/caulk job
before it becomes the $5,000 floor-joist issue *or* — worse for your wallet — becomes
the deposit deduction that isn't your fault but looks like it because nobody took
photos along the way.
